This edition picks up on October 20, where our September–October column left off, and covers the last twelve days of the month. It is late: the routine that writes it failed on November 1, and we would rather publish an accurate column three weeks late than a hurried one on time. In those twelve days the venture market produced nothing that cleared our verification bar, and three events said more about the shape of the stack than any round would have. A CRM bought a marketing engine, a standard left its author's hands, and the category's most expensive company told us what it is actually earning.

Salesforce buys the engagement layer

On October 20, Salesforce agreed to acquire Braze for about $5.66B in enterprise value, $47.50 a share in cash, according to Braze's own SEC filing. The deal is expected to close in Salesforce's fiscal 2028, which begins in February 2027, subject to Braze shareholder and regulatory approval. Termination fees run both ways: $300M if Salesforce walks, $191.4M if Braze does.

Read it alongside the rest of Salesforce's year and the pattern is hard to miss. Qualified (an inbound AI SDR) closed in April, Fin (customer-service agents) in September, Contentful (content) on September 1, and now Braze for real-time customer engagement. That is an agentic front office assembled by acquisition, piece by piece. The open question for buyers is Marketing Cloud: Salesforce has not said how Braze will sit alongside it, and that is a reason not to sign a long Marketing Cloud renewal until it does. The deal is recorded on our funding tracker as pending, and the full sequence is on the Salesforce profile.

Agent Skills becomes a standard nobody owns

On October 22, at AGNTCon + MCPCon North America, the Agentic AI Foundation announced that Anthropic had contributed its Agent Skills standard to the foundation, as one of four new projects alongside contributions from OpenAI and Hugging Face. The foundation now holds the Agent Skills name, its GitHub organisation and the agentskills.io domain.

This matters more for GTM teams than it sounds. A skill written once — how to research an account, how to score a lead against your ICP, how to write back to your CRM — can now travel between agent runtimes without being rewritten for each vendor. That is the same argument we have been making about the agent-native stack: build on protocols, not platforms. It is also, to be precise, a further step toward an open standard rather than the third-party skills marketplace we once predicted, and we are not grading it as one.

Sierra doubles again

At the end of the month Sierra said it had passed $400M in annual recurring revenue, adding more than $150M in the third quarter alone and doubling from $200M in about five months (company-stated, on Sierra's blog; coverage dates the announcement to October 30). At the $15.8B valuation set in May, that is roughly forty times ARR, down from the near-eighty times its May disclosure implied.

The number changes the argument about Sierra more than any funding round could. Our customer-agents analysis has questioned whether the May valuation was paying for dominance the company had not yet earned; on its own figures it is now earning a good share of it. The caution is unchanged: this is a company-stated figure from a private company, not an audited one. See the updated Sierra profile.

Also in the window

On October 29, Pipedrive's CEO Paulo Cunha announced a proposed restructuring he called the largest structural change in the company's history: fewer teams, layers and locations, a workforce reduction affecting most roles, and a pivot to being "product-led." It follows September's launch of Nova, AI meeting intelligence included in every plan. For the many small teams on Pipedrive, nothing changes on day one; it is worth watching whether the product cadence holds through the reorganisation.
